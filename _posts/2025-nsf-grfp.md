In the summer of 2025, I was awarded the NSF-GRFP. The NSF-GRFP is a fellowship for PhD students, but if you're reading this, I'm assuming you already know what it is. After using countless resources, and retroactively serving on a panel and sharing my materials with applicants, I decided to write a blog post with my advice & materials.

I had applied for the NSF-GRFP twice, once during the year before I started grad school, and once during my first year of grad school. Obviously, I was rejected the first time (or else I wouldn't have applied again). On my first try, I'll be honest--I didn't have anyone read my materials (I was in the middle of a gap year, and wasn't actively engaging with my professors). Nonetheless, I was able to get some helpful feedback by the time the NSF-GRFP reviews came around. Feel free to reach out to me if you want to see my reviews & earlier materials!

I'm going to skip the advice that you've probably heard on every other NSF-GRFP-oriented webpage/blogpost you've visited. To be brief, that advice includes 1. start early, 2. have many other people read your materials, 3. write & rewrtie (many iterations), 4. include titles for "intellectual merit" and "broader impacts", etc. Onto my (hopefully more niche advice):

At the time I was preparing my application, I had little to know idea what I was doing (at least research-wise). In my first application, I was without help, and wrote my research proposal about some research I had done in undergrad.

While doing my second application, I had just started a PhD program in Computer Science, after completing my B.S. in pure math (no, I was not good at coding). Much of my familiarity with my current field came from reading I did during Fall 2025 (at the same time I was applying for the NSF-GRFP). I had no clue how to write a proposal in this field, nor did I have any idea what was interesting or had already been done.

I am extremely grateful for my advisor, who helped come up with ideas on what to write my research proposal on. That being said, I think reviewers do care more about your ability to write a research proposal, rather than the nitty-gritty details about what research is being proposed. In my opinion, they want to see that you have the potential to be a good research. Therefore,

### Anticipate and address challenges

As I elluded to before, at the time of my second application, I had no idea what I was doing. Believe it or not, this makes it pretty hard to write a research proposal. Even when I knew what I was writing about, I had no idea if an experiment I was proposing was realistic, if the timeline of my project was realistic at all, or if it even made sense. While these points should be refined when writing your statement, no one will be able to get this just right.

I think one of the things that makes a research proposal stronger is to anticipate and address challenges you might encounter: generally, if X happens, I'll re-evaluate Y and then do Z. Maybe your problem definition does not make sense, and you have to make more assumptions, or maybe you realize your experiment will tak way too long, and you have to make some modification. Whatever it may be, it's helpful if you notice and point it out in your application.
